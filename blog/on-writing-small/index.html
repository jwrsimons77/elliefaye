On Writing Small

June 2026 ✦ 5 min read ✦ On craft

Seven years in agencies taught me one thing worth keeping: the shortest line in the room is usually the loudest.

There is a version of copywriting that wants to be seen doing the work. It stacks adjectives like a nervous waiter stacking plates. It says "innovative solutions" when it means "we do a thing," and "passionate about people" when it means nothing at all. I wrote plenty of it. Everyone does, at first. It feels safe, the way shouting feels safe in a room where nobody is listening.

Poetry cured me of it — not because poetry is small, but because poetry is accountable. In a poem, every word turns up to its own trial. There is nowhere for "solutions" to hide. You learn quickly that a line survives on what it removes: the poem about grief never says grief; the ad that moves you never says moving.

White space isn't the absence of copy. It's the part of the sentence the reader writes themselves.

The best brief I ever answered was four words long. The best line I ever wrote lost eleven of its fifteen words across three rounds of review — and got better every round, which was humbling in the way being wrong in public is humbling. Restraint isn't a style. It's a kind of respect. It assumes the reader is quick, busy, and allergic to being sold to, because they are, because you are.

So now I write small on purpose. I give the idea one clean sentence to stand in, and I stop. If the sentence wobbles, I don't add scaffolding — I find a better sentence. The delete key is the only tool in this job that never lies to you.

✦ ✦ ✦

A practical ending, because craft essays owe you one. Before anything ships, I read it out loud on an imaginary bus. If a stranger would look up — keep it. If they'd look away — cut it. If they'd get off a stop early, start again.

— E.F.
